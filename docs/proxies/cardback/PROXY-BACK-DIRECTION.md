# The proxy back: a direction

*Written 2026-10-12, before anything was painted. One back for every proxy card
Justin prints, in every cube, inside clear sleeves. Cube-specific art goes on
sleeves later, not here.*

## What the back has to do

- **Be his.** It is not a fantasy back from any game. It belongs to one thing: a
  collection he builds by hand, drafts with one close friend across a table at
  home, and plays with a coach looking over both shoulders. The strongest
  material here is not "fantasy". It is **the table and the two of them at it**.
- **Read as "our own deck" at a glance.** It should look like a crafted object
  with its own history: a bound book, an inlaid box, a painted panel. Nobody
  should mistake it for a real card back. Once the style says that, no word has
  to.
- **Look the same from both seats.** A back with an obvious "up" tells the other
  player which way a card went into a hand. So every concept is 180° symmetric,
  or reads the same turned around. That rule also hands us the motif. **There
  are two players, and each one sees the back from their own side.** A design
  that turns into itself is a picture of the two seats.
- **Survive printing.** That means 600 identical copies on S33 stock behind
  sleeve glare. The darkest large areas sit around RGB 20–30, never pure black.
  Gold is mid-toned (ochre-brass, not yellow), with no neon. Detail stays at
  mid-level everywhere: big flat patches show every scuff and sleeve scratch,
  and hairline noise turns to mush at 300 dpi. The art bleeds to the edge
  (1632×2220 at 600 dpi). The cut is 75 px in on every side, and nothing that
  matters goes outside the 135 px safe line.
- **Borrow nothing.** That rules out a brown field with an oval centre, five
  coloured orbs, a swirling vortex frame, or anything lettered like Magic. No
  card art, no living artist's style. The concepts below draw on historical
  crafts (bookbinding, marquetry, court-card design, enamel, manuscript
  interlace, star charts). They are centuries old, and nobody owns them.

## The emblem, before the concepts

Three things recur, and each concept picks some of them:

- **The cube.** An isometric hexagon, the "tumbling block". It is the cube they
  draft, it is geometric rather than heraldic, and it belongs to nobody.
- **The two seats.** Two of anything, head-to-tail. That covers two figures, two
  hands, two strands or two constellations.
- **The lamp.** One small light between the two seats: the coach. It is a round
  light (an orb or a star), so it turns into itself.

## Words (the owner's update of 2026-10-12)

Words are allowed now, and the back may say what these are. No concept below
*needs* a word: each should already say "a deck of our own" through its style.
If words go on, the rules are:

- They are set afterwards in HTML/SVG, never painted (image models garble
  letters). Each concept leaves calm space where words would sit.
- They must not break the symmetry. Words go on **twice, head-to-tail**, like a
  playing card's indices. Or they go **twice around a ring**, which makes the
  ring legend genuinely 180° symmetric: "· A PROXY FOR FRIENDS · A PROXY FOR
  FRIENDS ·" turned around lands on itself.
- Keep it to one short line. Candidates: the collection's name, if Justin gives
  it one; *a proxy for friends*; *not for sale*. Each concept says where its
  words would go.

## Six concepts

### 1. Binder's Gilt

**The image.** A Renaissance book cover. The ground is deep bottle-green morocco
leather with a fine pebbled grain. Gold-tooled strapwork forms a double ruled
border with interlacing straps at the corners. A tall lozenge in the centre
holds a small gilt cube with a tooled star at its heart. Small gilt fleurons
(tooled leaves) are scattered evenly across the field.

**Why it has a life of its own.** A proxy collection is a book he binds himself,
and every card is a leaf of it. Gold on leather says "made by hand, kept with
care" and has nothing to do with any card game's back. Green keeps it far from
a brown back.

**Symmetry.** Bindings are mirror-symmetric on both axes by tradition, so 180°
comes for free.

**Words.** A tooled title panel at the top and bottom of the lozenge, set
head-to-tail. This is where the collection's name or *a proxy for friends*
would sit.

**Print risks.** Gold tooling can print as flat ochre, so the gold needs a
lit/shadow ramp. Leather grain is exactly the right mid-level texture to hide
scuffs. If the green goes muddy it reads brown, so keep it cool.

### 2. Tumbling Blocks

**The image.** The whole field is the three-tone cube illusion that quilters
call "tumbling blocks". It is done in marquetry: ebonised wood for the dark
faces, smoked green-grey for the mid faces, pale bone-coloured holly for the
light faces. Each block is about 2 cm across on the card. A brass stringing line
frames the field inside the safe area. In the centre, one block is inlaid in
gilt brass instead of wood.

**Why it has a life of its own.** It shows the cube, literally: hundreds of
cubes, one picked out in gold. A marquetry panel is assembled from cut pieces,
the way a proxy deck is. Nothing in it is a fantasy cliché, and it still feels
like an old games box.

**Symmetry.** The pattern is exactly 180° symmetric. A rhombus turned 180° keeps
its orientation, so each of the three shades maps onto itself and the tiling
lands on itself. The illusion reads the same either way.

**Words.** None wanted. If *not for sale* is ever needed, a doubled ring legend
can go around the gold block.

**Print risks.** The geometry has to be exact, because a warped tiling looks
broken. So the pattern is drawn in code and the model only adds the wood. The
pale faces are the brightest large areas on any of these backs, so the
contrast must stay moderate or the pale faces show grime. Wood grain gives
honest mid-level texture.

### 3. The Two Seats

**The image.** A double-ended court card in the old playing-card tradition. Each
half shows a half-length figure, a cloaked card player holding a fan of cards.
The bottom half is the same figure turned 180°. Between them, on the line where
the halves meet, sits a table edge and a round glass lamp: the coach's light,
lit for both of them. The medium is egg tempera on a gold-leaf ground, flat and
decorative, in the manner of 15th-century painted cards, with deep teal, garnet
and ochre.

**Why it has a life of its own.** This is the most personal concept: the back is a
portrait of how the cards are actually played, by two people across one table
with a light between them. It looks like no game's back, and the double-ended
court is a centuries-old public convention, nobody's trade dress.

**Symmetry.** True 180° by construction: one half, rotated.

**Words.** None needed. If wanted, one small line can run along the table edge
on each side, each reading toward its own seat.

**Print risks.** Faces and hands are where the model fails. The figures stay
stylised and small-featured, and the hands are mostly hidden behind the card
fan. Gold-leaf ground plus dark cloaks gives good mid-level contrast. The seam
needs a feathered blend through the lamp and table.

### 4. Cloisonné Rose

**The image.** An enamel rosette on a dark field. Gold wire cells hold deep
jewel enamels, with the petals alternating between two colours (teal and
garnet, one for each player). There is a gilt cube at the hub. The ground is
dark blue-black enamel with a scatter of small gold stars.

**Why it has a life of its own.** It is a jewel, and a jewel is a strong
identity, but the rose window and the mandala are the most worn emblems in
fantasy.

**Symmetry.** Radial, so trivially symmetric.

**Words.** Optional: a doubled ring legend around the rose.

**Print risks.** Enamel highlights tend toward neon saturation. The large dark
ground is the scuff risk; the stars help.

### 5. Two Threads (carpet page)

**The image.** An insular-manuscript carpet page. Two interlaced strands knot
around a lozenge without ever separating: two players, one game. Bands are
ochre and teal on a deep ink ground, with a stepped border.

**Why it has a life of its own.** The idea is lovely, and the medium is public
domain and unmistakably hand-made.

**Symmetry.** Interlace is easily made 180° symmetric.

**Words.** None.

**Print risks.** Real interlace has to be geometrically correct (over-under
alternating), and the model produces spaghetti. Doing it well means drawing the
whole knot in code, at which point it is a vector design with texture on top.
Manuscripts also invite pseudo-script, the letter problem in its worst form.

### 6. The Night Table (star chart)

**The image.** A celestial chart in engraved gold on deep blue. Two
constellations sit head-to-tail, each a figure of a seated player, with a pole
star between them (the coach). There are concentric graticule circles and a
faint cloud of small stars.

**Why it has a life of its own.** The meaning is good, and star charts read as
"old and wondrous".

**Symmetry.** Easy to make 180° symmetric.

**Words.** Charts are full of lettering, which this concept would leave out.

**Print risks.** The model fills charts with zodiac glyphs, numerals and
lettering, which are text by another name. Large dark sky means scuffs.
Star specks are the fine noise that mushes at 300 dpi.

## The three I am painting, and why

1. **Binder's Gilt.** It gives the best read of "a collection of our own" with
   the least explaining. Its texture is the most print-robust of the six, and
   it has a natural, calm home for words if Justin wants them.
2. **Tumbling Blocks.** It puts the cube on the back without a single
   pictogram, its symmetry is provable rather than approximate, and it is the
   most original back of the six. It is also the most likely to survive 600
   prints unchanged, because the geometry is drawn rather than hoped for.
3. **The Two Seats.** It is the riskiest to execute and the one that means the
   most. It is the only concept whose subject is the two of them at the table,
   and its symmetry is the playing card's own answer to the orientation problem.
   If the figures fail, this is the one to repaint, not the one to drop.

The Cloisonné Rose is the runner-up: safe, handsome and least his. Two Threads
and the Night Table both lose to the letter problem: their media invite exactly
the glyphs the model cannot be trusted with.

## How they are painted

All three are painted on local SDXL only. A layout guide is drawn in code first
(`tools/` in the work folder: borders, lozenge, tiling, the two seats' masses)
and painted over with img2img at moderate strength. That keeps the centre
centred and the geometry exact. Symmetry is then enforced: mirror quadrants for
the binding, 180° rotation for the tiling and the court card, with a feathered
seam. Every final is 1632×2220, and its `.txt` records the model, prompt, seed,
steps, cfg, size, upscale and the measured darkest areas. The results and what
went wrong are at the end of this file once painted.

## What was painted (2026-10-12)

Nine backs, three per concept, in [`direction/`](direction/). Each is a 1632×2220
PNG at 600 dpi with a `.txt` beside it (model, prompt, negative, seed, steps, cfg,
size, upscale, symmetry, the measured darkest areas and its history).
[`CONTACT-SHEET.jpg`](direction/CONTACT-SHEET.jpg) shows the bare backs.
[`CONTACT-SHEET-cut-safe.jpg`](direction/CONTACT-SHEET-cut-safe.jpg) draws the
75 px cut line (red) and the 135 px safe line (blue) on copies.
[`direction/tools/`](direction/tools/) holds the scripts and the per-round specs.
They ran from a work folder at `~/scenery-packs/proxyback-work`, so their paths
are relative to that.

**Settings.** All nine use `Lykon/dreamshaper-xl-1-0` (fp16) with
`madebyollin/sdxl-vae-fp16-fix`. Every image is img2img over a guide drawn in
code, sampled with DPM++ 2M Karras for 32 steps at cfg 6 (denoising strength
per file), with classic attention on the GTX 1080. Generated sizes were 832×1152
for a whole back and 1216×832 for one half of the court card. Each was
Lanczos-upscaled to 1632 wide and cover-cropped to 1632×2220, with a light
unsharp mask. Chroma above 140 is compressed so nothing prints neon. Darkest
areas are measured on luminance blurred to about 0.5 mm. Six GPU rounds took
about 41 minutes in total, model loading included, all under the shared GPU
lock.

**What went wrong, and what was redone.**

- **Round 1 failed on all three.** The binder painted as plain, flat leather.
  The blocks came out as flat paint, not wood. The court figure came out as a
  faceless cut-out. Each guide was redrawn:
  - the binder in the *à l'éventail* manner (fan corner pieces, a fan rosette,
    a roll border, a semé of stars);
  - the blocks with veneer grain drawn per face;
  - the figure with shading and a face, then scaled to 84% because its head
    had run into the cut line.
- **The binders' Necker cube was lost twice.**
  - The model dissolved it into a rosette. It was redrawn and repainted in at
    strength 0.30.
  - My own post step then mirrored it into a crossed octagon. A Necker cube
    turns 180° onto itself but is not mirror-symmetric. The centre is now kept
    as painted and averaged with its own rotation, while the rest of the
    binding is mirrored.
- **Two binders were partly regenerated for letter-like marks.** On the green
  and garnet, the corner arabesques painted as calligraphic glyphs that read
  like script. The guide's fan corners were pasted back and repainted (round
  4). That pass made the leather streaky, so those two finals take round 3's
  leather and only round 4's corners.
- **The model would not paint a fan of cards.** At strength 0.80 it painted a
  monk against wood panelling, and in one case real playing cards with pips,
  which was rejected outright. At 0.72 it painted a candle, a folding fan and a
  lampshade. The guide's card fan, hands and round lamp were pasted back and
  repainted at 0.50. A pass at 0.38 stayed flat, like clip-art, and was
  dropped. That 0.50 pass also gave the gold ground its streaked, mosaic-like
  texture.
- **Every final was checked** in crops at about 2/3 scale over the half or
  quadrant that its symmetry repeats, and at full size at the corners, the
  centre and the court card's seam. None carries letters, glyphs or a
  signature-like mark.

**Where words would go.**

- **Binders' Gilt** has two calm, gilt-ruled cartouches, at the top and at the
  bottom. The words go in them head-to-tail.
- **The Two Seats**, if anything, takes one short line along each side of the
  table band, each reading toward its own seat.
- **Tumbling Blocks** wants no words.

**My read, for choosing.**

- **Binders' Gilt 2 (blue)** is the most finished and the most print-safe of
  the nine.
- **Tumbling Blocks** is the most original. It is also the only one whose
  symmetry is exact by construction. Its pale faces make it the brightest
  back, so it will show grime first.
- **The Two Seats** is the one with a story, and it works as a double-ended
  court card. Its execution is the weakest: model faces, a flat lamp and one
  bad hand. If it is the one Justin loves, it deserves a careful repaint, not
  this direction pass.

## The Two Seats — the direction (2026-10-13)

Justin, on round 7 of the sleeve art (`two-seats/round7/`), in his own words:

> "This looks better. Let's build in this direction. The idea might be a bit of
> implied humor in these oil-painting-esque style images on less than impressive
> characters in a card game, and there's irony of them being on playing cards as
> well, while echoing European fine art."

That is now the art direction for The Two Seats. These are the rules that got
us here, and every later round keeps them.

**Three different, unremarkable people.** The green woman, the garnet man and
the blue man are ordinary faces with real human expressions: calm, worried,
guarded. They are the faces from the first drafts, and they are never
regenerated. A new round may touch everything around a face, but never the face.

**The gravity of a European portrait, spent on an evening of cards.** The joke
is in the mismatch. The sitters are posed and lit as if for a court painter,
with a gold ground and a gilt frame, and what they are doing is holding a hand
of cards across a table. The picture never winks at that. It takes them as
seriously as they take the game.

**Everything but the faces is drawn flat.** The hands, arms, chests, the fan
of cards and anything set on the table are drawn in the court-card line style:
flat fills, a second flat tone for shade, and a bold dark outline. Photoreal
hands and painterly passes over the drawing were both tried and both rejected.
The faces and hoods stay painted, and the drawn parts share one printed surface
with them through a faint, even paper grain, so that the card reads as one print
and not as two techniques.

**Round 2's setting.** The gold ground, the floral border, the walnut table
band and the round lamp at its centre are round 2's pixels, and they stay.

**The fan is held with both hands at the centre.** It is a modest spread of
plain cards, round 2's dark backs with gilt edges, held together at the chest.
The cards carry no pips, letters or marks.

**Exact 180° symmetry.** The bottom seat is the top half turned 180°, pixel for
pixel. Anything added is added to the top half and turns with it.

**Humour by small, deadpan detail, never caricature.** The faces are not pulled
into jokes, and nothing is exaggerated. What is funny is quiet: a neat stack of
coins in front of one sitter, a cold cup and one lone coin in front of another.
Nothing should read as a gag from across the room. It is found up close, the way
a detail in an old painting is.

**Words are optional, and set later in SVG.** If the back carries words, they
are set as type over the finished art, never painted into it.
